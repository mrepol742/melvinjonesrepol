The recent reports surrounding the alleged unauthorized access to confidential banking records involving Zenith Bank caught my attention because it highlights something that many cybersecurity courses simplify a little too much. We learn the CIA Triad almost immediately when studying security, and for good reason because confidentiality, integrity, and availability are fundamental concepts. But after reading enough incident reports over the years, I started noticing that real attacks rarely fit inside three clean boxes. Sometimes the systems are still online, the data has not been modified, and yet the organization is dealing with legal problems, reputation damage, investigations, and a complete loss of customer confidence. That is a security incident, even if the CIA checklist does not immediately scream "critical failure."

Based on publicly reported court filings, the allegations involve unauthorized access to confidential financial records and the disclosure of sensitive banking information rather than an attack that brought banking services offline or corrupted databases. The matter is currently part of legal proceedings, so the final facts will ultimately be determined by the courts.

## A Quick Refresher on the CIA Triad

Most of us know the three pillars.

* **Confidentiality** protects information from unauthorized disclosure.
* **Integrity** ensures information remains accurate and unmodified.
* **Availability** keeps systems and services accessible.

The framework is still incredibly useful. It provides a simple way to classify many security objectives, and it has survived decades because it works.

The problem begins when people assume every cyber incident can be fully explained by only these three concepts.

## What Actually Happened?

According to publicly available reports, investigators allege that confidential financial records belonging to a company were accessed, transmitted, and disclosed without authorization using banking computer infrastructure. Those allegations resulted in criminal charges under Nigeria's Cybercrimes Act. The legal process is still ongoing, so the outcome has not yet been determined.

At first glance, many people immediately say:

> "That's just confidentiality."

Technically, that is true.

But practically, it is incomplete.

## Why This Doesn't Fit Neatly Into the CIA Triad

One thing I have learned from reading breach reports is that organizations rarely lose sleep because someone violated a textbook definition.

They lose sleep because of everything that comes after.

Imagine a customer discovers their confidential financial records have been accessed without permission. Even if every database entry remains perfectly accurate and every banking service continues running normally, several things have already happened.

Customers begin questioning whether they should continue trusting the institution.

Regulators start asking difficult questions.

Internal investigations consume hundreds or thousands of work hours.

Legal costs begin piling up.

Public confidence starts dropping.

None of those are directly represented by confidentiality, integrity, or availability.

The technical breach might be confidentiality.

The business impact is much larger.

## Security Is Also About Trust

One thing security frameworks sometimes struggle to express is trust.

Trust is difficult to measure.

You cannot graph it like CPU usage.

You cannot patch it with a security update.

Once customers begin wondering whether their information is truly private, rebuilding that confidence often takes far longer than fixing the technical issue.

Ironically, many organizations recover their infrastructure much faster than they recover their reputation.

## Modern Security Frameworks Go Beyond CIA

This is one reason many organizations no longer rely exclusively on the CIA Triad when designing security programs.

They also think about things like:

* Privacy
* Accountability
* Auditability
* Non-repudiation
* Authenticity
* Compliance
* Resilience
* Business continuity
* Customer trust

Take privacy as an example.

The CIA Triad says confidential information should remain confidential.

Privacy asks a different question.

**Should this information have been accessible in the first place, even internally?**

That is a governance question as much as a technical one.

## Banks Have Always Known This

Interestingly, Zenith Bank's own public cybersecurity and governance reports discuss much broader objectives than simply protecting confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

They mention data privacy certifications, employee awareness training, operational resilience, governance, encryption, disaster recovery, compliance programs, and data loss prevention technologies. Those are all pieces of a modern security strategy because financial institutions understand that protecting customer trust requires much more than preventing downtime.

In other words, banks themselves already recognize that cybersecurity extends beyond the original CIA model.

---

The CIA Triad is still one of the best teaching tools in cybersecurity.

I would never argue that it should disappear.

What I would argue is that it should be treated as the foundation instead of the entire building.

Real incidents remind us that security is not only about whether a server stayed online or whether a database was modified. It is also about governance, human behavior, legal responsibility, privacy, regulatory obligations, and the confidence customers place in an organization every single day.

The Zenith Bank case is another reminder that cybersecurity is rarely just a technical problem. Sometimes the hardest thing to restore is not a compromised system. It is the trust that quietly disappeared the moment confidential information left the place where it was supposed to stay.
