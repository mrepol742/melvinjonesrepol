I woke up to one of those technology headlines that initially sounds like somebody made it up for engagement: Donald Trump wants artificial intelligence to be called "super intelligence." Except this time, it came directly from a speech by the President of the United States at the United Nations. Trump argued that the word "artificial" makes intelligence sound fake, while the technology itself is very real and increasingly capable. He said U.S. government documents would use "super intelligence," or SI, going forward. As somebody who works with software and AI-powered systems, the first thing that came to my mind was not politics. It was the naming problem we just inherited if this terminology actually sticks.

There is something almost developer-like about looking at an established name and deciding, "Nope, we're renaming this." Anyone who has renamed a variable because the old one bothered them can probably understand the impulse. The problem is that `superintelligence` is not exactly an unused variable name.

## What Trump Actually Said

During his September 22, 2026 address to the United Nations General Assembly, Trump said that artificial intelligence should instead be referred to as "super intelligence." His reasoning was fairly straightforward: he believes the word "artificial" makes the intelligence sound fake, while he considers the technology to be real and significant.

He described the proposed terminology as "SI" and said U.S. documents would use the new wording. He also encouraged the rest of the world to adopt it.

This was not just a random alternative phrase mentioned once during the speech. Trump connected the terminology to his broader position that the United States should continue aggressively developing AI rather than slowing the industry down. Days earlier, he had also announced plans for an "AI Force" and argued against restricting the industry's growth.

That makes the announcement more interesting than a simple name change.

## There Is One Slight Problem

"Superintelligence" already means something.

That is where things become confusing from a technical perspective.

Artificial intelligence is an enormous umbrella. A recommendation system can be AI. A computer vision model can be AI. A language model can be AI. The autocomplete system trying to guess what I am typing can technically involve AI.

Superintelligence traditionally refers to something much more specific: intelligence exceeding human capabilities across important intellectual domains. Reporting on Trump's announcement also pointed out this existing distinction.

Those two concepts are definitely not interchangeable.

Calling every AI system "super intelligence" would be a little like deciding that every computer should now be called a supercomputer.

Technically, my machine is a computer.

It is not suddenly a supercomputer because I renamed the variable.

## AI, AGI, and Superintelligence Are Different Ideas

The terminology around AI is already messy enough without adding another collision.

When developers say **AI**, we can be talking about a huge collection of technologies and systems. Machine learning, computer vision, natural language processing, recommendation engines, generative models, autonomous agents, and plenty of other things fit somewhere underneath that umbrella.

Then there is **AGI**, or artificial general intelligence. There is no universally agreed technical threshold for AGI, but the term generally describes systems capable of performing a broad range of intellectual tasks rather than being specialized around one narrow capability.

And then there is **superintelligence**.

That usually represents another step entirely: systems whose intellectual capabilities significantly exceed those of humans.

Researchers are actively debating whether increasingly autonomous AI development could eventually lead toward systems capable of improving their own successors. Recent reporting has highlighted work at major AI labs, including Anthropic and OpenAI, around increasingly capable AI systems that can contribute to AI research itself.

So when somebody says "superintelligence" in an AI research conversation, there is already a lot packed into that word.

Using it as a replacement for AI changes the meaning considerably.

## The Developer in Me Immediately Thinks About Compatibility

Imagine this terminology actually becomes common across U.S. government systems.

Now documentation starts saying "super intelligence systems."

Policies mention "SI models."

Government contracts request "SI capabilities."

Meanwhile, developers, researchers, academic papers, APIs, SDKs, documentation, and probably the rest of the industry continue using "AI."

Congratulations. We have invented terminology compatibility issues.

It reminds me of maintaining an API after somebody decides that an existing field name is no longer descriptive enough. You can rename it, sure. But now every consumer needs to understand whether the old and new names represent the same thing.

Except this time the deprecated identifier is `AI`, and the codebase is basically the entire technology industry.

## I Understand the Argument Behind the Name

There is still an interesting point hiding underneath the unusual terminology.

The word "artificial" can make something sound synthetic or fake in everyday conversation. Trump specifically argued that this description does not match how significant the technology has become.

I can understand that interpretation even if I would not rename the technology because of it.

When we say artificial intelligence, "artificial" does not mean imaginary intelligence. It means intelligence produced or simulated by machines rather than biological intelligence.

Artificial lighting is still real light.

Artificial satellites are still real satellites.

Artificial intelligence can produce very real results while remaining artificial.

The existing terminology is actually useful because it describes where that capability comes from.

## The Bigger Part of the Speech Wasn't Really the Name

The name generated the funny headline, but Trump's broader message about AI matters more.

He argued that the United States should continue encouraging development rather than heavily restricting it, while also saying the technology needs to be watched carefully. He framed leadership in AI, or SI under his proposed terminology, as strategically important in competition with China.

That debate is much bigger than whether we call something AI.

Developers are currently building systems capable of generating software, operating tools, researching information, finding vulnerabilities, automating workflows, and completing tasks that would have sounded ridiculous not very long ago.

At the same time, increasingly capable systems introduce legitimate questions about security, reliability, employment, misuse, model autonomy, and how much control humans should retain.

You do not solve those problems by renaming the technology.

You also do not solve them by pretending progress can simply be switched off.

That uncomfortable middle ground is where most of the interesting engineering work lives.

## From a Developer's Perspective

I use AI as a tool.

That distinction matters to me.

I can ask a model to analyze something, generate code, explain unfamiliar code, suggest architecture, find mistakes, or automate repetitive work. But I still have to understand what comes back. Sometimes the answer is excellent. Sometimes it confidently invents something that does not exist and leaves me wondering whether I made the mistake.

Anyone who has worked with AI-assisted development long enough has probably experienced that moment.

You ask for a library method.

The model gives you a beautiful implementation.

You paste it.

The compiler looks at you.

The method never existed.

Very super.

Much intelligence.

That does not make AI useless. Quite the opposite. These systems have become some of the most useful development tools I have available. It just means capability and reliability are not the same measurement.

Calling today's entire AI ecosystem "super intelligence" risks making the technology sound more universally capable than it actually is.

## Names Actually Matter in Technology

Developers spend an unreasonable amount of time naming things because names create expectations.

A function called `deleteUser()` should probably delete a user.

A boolean called `isAuthenticated` should hopefully tell me whether somebody is authenticated.

And something called **superintelligence** naturally creates a much stronger expectation than something called artificial intelligence.

Terminology also affects how ordinary users understand technology.

If every chatbot, recommendation algorithm, image generator, coding assistant, and machine-learning system suddenly becomes "super intelligence," the phrase stops describing a particular level of capability. It becomes branding.

That would be unfortunate because we may eventually need the original meaning.

If genuinely superintelligent systems are ever created, we probably want a word available to distinguish them from the chatbot helping someone rewrite an email.

## Maybe AI Is Fine

Technology names rarely describe their subjects perfectly.

Java has very little to do with coffee.

JavaScript is not Java.

Cloud computing mostly involves somebody else's physical computers sitting inside very real buildings.

Cookies are considerably less delicious than their name suggests.

And yet we somehow survive.

Artificial intelligence has decades of research, terminology, standards, documentation, education, products, APIs, and public understanding built around those two letters.

AI.

It is short. Everyone recognizes it. Developers know roughly what somebody means when they say it, even though the category itself is enormous.

Replacing it with SI creates another problem because superintelligence already describes something potentially far beyond the systems most of us are using today.

Maybe the funniest outcome would be developers continuing to call everything AI while government documents call it SI. Software has survived worse naming decisions.

For now, I think I'll keep `AI` in my variable names.

Refactoring the entire industry sounds like a terrible pull request.
